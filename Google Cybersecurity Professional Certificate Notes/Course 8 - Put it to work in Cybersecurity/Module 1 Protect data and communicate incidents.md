Data Classification Types

- **Public data** is accessible to the public and poses minimal risk, but still requires basic security. Examples include press releases and job descriptions.
- **Private data** has a higher security level and should be kept from the public, as unauthorized access can pose a serious risk. Examples include company email addresses and employee identification numbers.
- **Sensitive data** must be protected from unauthorized access due to potential significant damage to finances and reputation. This includes personally identifiable information (PII) and protected health information (PHI).
- **Confidential data** is crucial for ongoing business operations and has limited access, often requiring non-disclosure agreements (NDAs). Examples include trade secrets and financial records.

Asset Classification

- **Low-level assets** like public data, if compromised, will not significantly impact an organization. A company's website address is an example.
- **High-level assets** such as sensitive and confidential data can have a significantly negative impact if leaked, leading to loss of competitive edge, reputation, and customer trust. An internal email discussing trade secrets is an example.

The Impact of Security Incidents

- Security incidents can lead to significant financial losses, regulatory fines, and damage to an organization's credibility.
- Protecting company data and assets is crucial due to these potential negative consequences.

Collaboration and Stakeholders in Security

- Security is a collaborative effort involving many individuals across an organization, each with specific security interests.
- Various stakeholders, including those focused on financial data, user credentials, vendor security, and employee PII, rely on the security team to protect the organization from attacks.

The Importance of Data Protection and Incident Management

- Protecting customer data, such as credit card numbers and personal information, is a primary concern, as customers trust organizations to safeguard their data.
- A security event is categorized as a security incident only if it results in a data breach; otherwise, it is not considered an incident.

**Four-Part Security Process**

- Security teams first identify and protect critical assets from potential threats.
- They then implement tools and processes to detect threats before creating business continuity and disaster recovery plans.

**Business Continuity Plan**

- This plan outlines procedures to sustain business operations during and after significant disruptions.
- Key steps include conducting a business impact analysis, identifying recovery steps for critical functions, organizing a dedicated team, and conducting training.

**Disaster Recovery Plan**

- This plan details the steps to minimize the impact of a security incident and resolve the threat.
- It includes strategies for restoring software and hardware functionality, and identifying impacted applications and data.

Understanding the Security Mindset

- A security mindset involves the ability to evaluate risk and continuously identify potential or actual breaches in systems, applications, or data.
- It requires recognizing what you are defending (assets) and who or what you are defending against (threats, risks, and vulnerabilities).

Applying the Security Mindset

- Staying informed about the latest security threats and vulnerabilities is essential to proactively protect company data.
- This mindset helps security analysts defend against constant attacks and prepare for worst-case scenarios, even if they don't occur.

Impact of a Security Mindset

- A strong security mindset enables the protection of all levels of assets, from guest Wi-Fi networks to intellectual property and financial information.
- Cultivating this mindset can differentiate you from other candidates when entering the security profession and can be a valuable talking point in job interviews.
